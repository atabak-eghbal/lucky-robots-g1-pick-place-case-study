Building an autonomous humanoid controller in simulation is as much about managing computational invariants as it is about control theory. These lessons capture the critical failures and insights from the development of the G1 baseline.

Technical Breakthroughs

Failure Mode

The Simulation Release NaN

Restoring object collisions immediately upon grip release caused catastrophic energy spikes (NaN in QACC). The fingers hadn't physically cleared the cylinder, leading to massive overlap impulses.

Correction

Delayed Collision Restoration

Introducing a 15-tick (~0.075s) delay between kinematic release and physical collision restoration solved the stability problem. This "grace period" allows the fingers to open enough to clear the cylinder geoms safely.

Insight

Sweet-Spot Navigation

Humanoids are asymmetrical in their manipulation workspace. Offsetting the target approach by +0.15m (world X) kept the drop point in the right arm's natural workspace, preventing overextension and improving release accuracy.

Fundamental Insights

Failure Mode

The Normalization Trap

The walker ONNX policy already applied internal normalization. Adding external normalization double-normalized observations, destabilized the gait, and caused catastrophic drift in the joint deltas.

Design Decision

The Always-On Reacher

The walker observes arm joint positions, so the right reacher had to run unconditionally. Keeping the arm in a known carry pose prevented out-of-distribution walking behavior and allowed the locomotion policy to function as trained.

Limitation

The Reacher Accuracy Floor

The right reacher has a practical accuracy floor of ~12 cm in this simulation context. FSM thresholds and timeout fallbacks were tuned around this empirical floor rather than assuming perfect convergence.

Physical Grasp Lesson from Steps 24–26

Physical contact is a different problem than task sequencing

  • FSM sequencing can be correct while grasp physics still fails.
  • Contact detection alone is not enough; closure speed, wrist drift, object size, and friction all matter.
  • Continuous grip control is better than binary closure, but still needs stable hand-object geometry.
  • Evidence-first metrics prevented us from falsely labeling the physical grasp as solved.
